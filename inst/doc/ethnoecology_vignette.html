A Critical Perspective on Ethnoecology Indices

Quantitative ethnoecology is the study of the use and importance of landscape units and ecosystems in human cultures, using statistical and analytical methods to quantify and interpret data. This approach has emerged as a key tool for understanding the cultural and ecological significance of landscape units, and for evaluating the impact of human activities on ecosystems.

While widely used in the literature, traditional ethnoecology indices have significant limitations that researchers should consider:

  • Oversimplification: Complex cultural-ecological relationships reduced to single numbers
  • Context stripping: Remove cultural, ecological, and historical context
  • Methodological issues: Sensitive to sampling design and interview methods
  • Theoretical concerns: Often lack strong theoretical foundations

When to use these indices:

  • Preliminary exploratory analysis
  • Comparison with existing literature that uses similar methods
  • As input variables for more sophisticated models (not as final results)

When to avoid these indices:

  • As primary evidence for policy decisions
  • For cross-cultural comparisons without careful validation
  • As standalone measures of “importance”

The field of ethnoecology has a long history, with roots dating back to the early 20th century when researchers began to systematically study the relationships between humans and their environments. In recent decades, advances in statistical and computational techniques have enabled researchers to more rigorously and systematically analyze and interpret ethnoecological data, leading to a greater understanding of the complex and diverse ways in which landscapes are used and valued in different cultural and ecological contexts.

Today, quantitative ethnoecology is an interdisciplinary field that draws on a range of methods and approaches from the natural and social sciences, including anthropology, ecology, geography, and sociology. It is a rapidly growing field that is making significant contributions to our understanding of human-environment relationships, and to the conservation and management of ecosystems.

Using the ethnoecologyR package

The ethnoecologyR package offers quantitative tools to assess the cultural significance of landscape units based on informant consensus. The package closely follows several key papers on ethnoecology indices (Tardío and Pardo-de-Santayana 2008; Friedman et al. 1986; Reyes-García et al. 2006; Toledo 2002). The goal is to provide an easy-to-use platform for ethnoecologists to perform quantitative ethnoecology assessments.

Users are highly encouraged to familiarize themselves with ethnoecology theory (Berkes 2012; Toledo 2002) and social-ecological systems theory (Ostrom 2009). An overview of this theoretical background will be helpful in understanding approaches in ethnoecology and formulating useful research questions.

An example data set called ethnoecology_data is provided to show how standard quantitative ethnoecology data should be formatted to interface with the ethnoecologyR package. This is an ethnoecology data set including one column of 15 knowledge holder identifiers informant and one of 6 landscape unit names landscape_unit. The rest of the columns are the identified ethnoecology use categories. The data in the use categories is populated with counts of uses per person (should be 0 or 1 values).

Many of the functions in ethnoecologyR make use of select() and filter() functions of the dplyr package (Wickham et al. 2026) and pipe functions %>% from the magrittr package (Bache and Wickham 2025). The intention is that these should be easy to understand and use, and that they should allow users the chance to pull the code for these functions and change anything they see fit.

Ethnoecology indices functions

Ethnoecology indices are numerical measures that are used to quantify the importance of landscape units in different cultural or ecological contexts. Some common ethnoecology indices are included here. These indices are often used to analyze ethnoecological data and to compare the relative importance of different landscape units within a particular culture or ecosystem.

The standard quantitative ethnoecology indices are probably too narrow a tool for a proper assessment of human and ecological interactions of interest. Still, they can be a useful entry way into understanding some aspects of human populations and how they interact with nature. The steps required to calculate these indices offer a way to quantify intangible factors of how human communities interact with the world. They can come in handy as additive pieces for more holistic assessments and analyses.

Landscape Use (LU)

The Landscape Use (LU) index is a measure of the overall importance of a landscape unit based on the total number of use reports. It is calculated as:

LU = sum of use reports for a landscape unit

Cultural Importance in Ecology (CIE)

The Cultural Importance in Ecology (CIE) index is a normalized measure of use intensity. It is calculated as:

CIE = LU / N, where N is the number of informants.

Frequency of Citation (FC)

Fidelity Level (FL)

The Fidelity Level (FL) is the percentage of informants who mention a landscape unit for a specific use category. It is calculated as:

FL = (number of informants mentioning the unit for a specific use / total number of informants mentioning the unit for any use) × 100

Cultural Value of Ecolandscape (CVe)

The Cultural Value of Ecolandscape (CVe) is a composite index combining LU and FC. It is calculated as:

CVe = (LU / N) × (FC / N)

Working with figures

Figures can help to visually represent the main ideas or key results of ethnoecology work. Here we walk through a few of the functions within the ethnoecologyR package for generating figures.

Chord diagrams with circlize

The following chord plots are made using functions from the circlize package (Gu 2022). An example of the application of chord plots in ethnoecology is described in a study on agrobiodiversity in Uganda (Whitney, Bahati, and Gebauer 2018).

The ethno_landscape_chord() function creates a chord diagram of ethnoecology uses and landscape units.

ethno_landscape_chord(ethnoecology_data, by = "landscape_unit")
Chord diagram of landscape units and uses

The function can also be used to create a chord diagram of ethnoecology uses and informants.

ethno_landscape_chord(ethnoecology_data, by = "informant")
Chord diagram of informants and uses

Alluvial plots with ggalluvial

The ethno_landscape_alluvial() function creates an alluvial diagram showing flows between informants, landscape units, and ecological uses.

ethno_landscape_alluvial(ethnoecology_data)
Alluvial plot of landscape uses

Radial plot with ggplot2

The landscape_radial_plot() function creates a radial bar plot to visualize multiple ethnoecology indices per landscape unit.

Bayesian Consensus Modeling

The ethnoecologyR package provides a Bayesian model (ethno_bayes_land) to estimate consensus among informants regarding landscape use and ecological knowledge. The model is fitted using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo via rstan (Stan Development Team 2024).

fit <- ethno_bayes_land(ethnoecology_data, iter = 500, chains = 2)
print(fit, pars = c("theta[1]", "theta[2]", "theta[3]"))

Conclusions

Quantitative ethnoecology offers a way to study the use and importance of landscape units in human cultures, using statistical and analytical methods to quantify and interpret data. This approach can be useful in a variety of contexts, including:

  • Understanding the cultural and ecological significance of landscape units
  • Evaluating the impact of human activities on ecosystems
  • Informing conservation and management strategies
  • Documenting local ecological knowledge (LEK)

One of the main benefits of quantitative ethnoecology is that it allows researchers to rigorously and systematically analyze and interpret data, providing more reliable and objective insights into the role of landscape units in different cultural and ecological settings.

Overall, the ethnoecologyR package provides a comprehensive set of tools for researchers and practitioners working in ethnoecology and related fields. By using these standardized methods and statistical analyses, researchers can more accurately compare and contrast the importance of different landscape units and evaluate the significance of their findings.
